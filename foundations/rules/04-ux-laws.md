# 04 · UX laws & psychological principles (U1–U30)
U1–U18 = the user's list; U19–U30 = the remaining entries on lawsofux.com. Deeper physics: `../design-laws-and-physics.md`.
[Source: lawsofux.com; uxmagic.ai/blog/laws-of-ux; myuxacademy.com/blog/ux-design-laws-principles (Jun 2026)]

| ID | Law | Apply it like this | Honest caveat |
|---|---|---|---|
| **U1** | **Fitts's Law**: time to hit a target depends on distance and size | Primary CTA large (≥ 44–48px tall) and near where attention already is; edge/corner placement on desktop; destructive actions small and far. | Applies to pointing. For keyboard users, focus order matters more. |
| **U2** | **Hick's Law**: decision time grows with number & complexity of choices | ≤ 7 top-level nav items; 3 pricing tiers; stage choices (one decision per step); highlight a recommended option. | Doesn't mean "fewer items always". Familiar, well-grouped long lists (country picker with search) are fine. |
| **U3** | **Jakob's Law**: users expect your site to work like others | Logo top-left → home, cart top-right, search in header, underlined links in text. Novel patterns must be tested before shipping. | Being conventional in *patterns* doesn't require being generic in *visuals*. Innovate in look, not in how controls work. |
| **U4** | **Miller's Law**: ~7±2 items in working memory | Use it as an argument for **chunking** (U20): phone numbers, codes, long forms in groups. | Often misapplied as "max 7 menu items". People scan menus; they don't memorise them. Later research suggests ~4 chunks. |
| **U5** | **Tesler's Law**: complexity is conserved; someone has to carry it | Move complexity to the system: smart defaults, auto-detect (card type, address lookup), sensible presets. | Hiding complexity can hide control. Expert modes still need access. |
| **U6** | **Doherty Threshold**: productivity soars when response < 400 ms | Acknowledge every action < 100 ms; optimistic UI; skeletons for 1–10 s; progress for > 10 s (H6 timing). | Perceived speed matters as much as real speed. Fake progress bars erode trust. |
| **U7** | **Postel's Law**: be liberal in what you accept, conservative in what you send | Accept "+92 300 1234567", "0300-1234567", "03001234567", then normalise. Accept dates in several formats; output one. | Lenient input must still validate meaning (an invalid card is still invalid). |
| **U8** | **Pareto Principle**: ~80% of effects from ~20% of causes | Put design effort on the top 20% of tasks/pages (analytics: top landing pages, top flows). | A heuristic, not a measured constant. |
| **U9** | **Peak-End Rule**: experiences judged by the peak and the end | Design the best moment (aha, reveal, success) and the end (confirmation, thank-you, receipt, offboarding) with extra care; soften error peaks. | Doesn't license bad middles; friction still loses people before they reach the end. |
| **U10** | **Von Restorff (isolation) effect**: the different item is remembered | **One** filled accent CTA per view; recommended plan visually distinct; never make everything "pop". | Accessibility: distinction can't rely on colour alone (C12). |
| **U11** | **Serial Position Effect**: first and last items remembered best | Most important nav items at the start and end; key benefit first; CTA at the end of long pages too. | Middle items still get found when navigation is well labelled. |
| **U12** | **Aesthetic-Usability Effect**: beautiful designs are perceived as easier to use | Invest in polish (spacing, type, imagery) because it buys tolerance. | It can **mask** usability problems in testing; observe behaviour, not opinions. |
| **U13** | **Zeigarnik Effect**: unfinished tasks are remembered | Show progress + remaining steps ("Profile 60% complete"); save drafts; resume where left off. | Nagging incompletion banners become dark patterns. |
| **U14** | **Occam's Razor**: prefer the simplest solution that works | Remove elements until the design breaks, then add one back. Delete ~30% of copy (DAE-UX deletion test). | Simplest for the *user*, not the builder. |
| **U15** | **Law of Proximity**: near things are grouped | Label close to its field (≤ 8px), groups separated by ≥ 2× inner spacing (L14). | Proximity beats colour and borders as a grouping cue. |
| **U16** | **Law of Similarity**: similar-looking things seem related | Same style = same function; links all look like links; don't style static text like buttons. | Similar looks with different behaviour break trust. |
| **U17** | **Law of Prägnanz**: people perceive the simplest form | Simple shapes for icons and logos; reduce visual noise; clean silhouettes in illustrations. | Over-simplified icons lose meaning, so add labels (H4). |
| **U18** | **Parkinson's Law**: tasks expand to fill the time available | Time-box steps; autofill; single-page short forms; estimated time ("2-minute setup"). | Countdown pressure is often a dark pattern. The Figma survey found shoppers notice being rushed. |
| **U19** | **Choice Overload**: too many options overwhelm | Curate (top picks, filters, comparison); default sort by relevance; "most popular". | The effect is context-dependent and smaller than early studies suggested. Experts want breadth. |
| **U20** | **Chunking**: group information into meaningful units | Steps, sections, card groups, formatted numbers (1 234 567), digit groups in codes. | Chunk by meaning, not by an arbitrary count. |
| **U21** | **Cognitive Load**: mental effort to use an interface | Remove extraneous load (decoration, jargon, inconsistent patterns); keep intrinsic load (the task) and support it with recognition (H4). | Some "load" is desirable engagement (games, learning). |
| **U22** | **Goal-Gradient Effect**: motivation rises near the goal | Progress bars that start partly filled (step 1 = account created); loyalty cards with 2 free stamps. | Endowed progress must be true; fake progress is deceptive. |
| **U23** | **Law of Common Region**: shared boundary = group | Use a background band or container to group **when space alone can't** (filters panel, pricing tier). | Every extra container adds visual weight; prefer space first (L6, L19). |
| **U24** | **Law of Uniform Connectedness**: visually connected = related | Lines/steps connecting a process; connectors in timelines; tabs attached to their panel. | Decorative connectors that imply false relationships confuse. |
| **U25** | **Mental Model**: users act on what they believe the system does | Use their vocabulary (from research/support tickets); mirror real-world flows (cart → checkout). | Mental models differ by segment; research, don't assume. |
| **U26** | **Paradox of the Active User**: users skip manuals and start doing | Inline guidance, empty states with a first action, contextual tooltips; no mandatory tours. | Experts still benefit from shortcuts and docs. |
| **U27** | **Selective Attention**: people filter what matches their goal (cf. banner blindness) | Don't style important content like ads (banners, carousels, flashing boxes); put key info in the content flow. | Auto-rotating carousels are routinely ignored. Make rotation user-controlled (A4). |
| **U28** | **Working Memory**: small, temporary store for the task | Keep needed info visible (order summary next to payment; comparison side by side); don't make users remember across screens. | Working memory differs by person and stress level; design for tired users. |
| **U29** | **Flow**: full immersion when challenge matches skill | Remove interruptions in core tasks (no pop-ups mid-flow); keyboard shortcuts for experts; instant feedback. | Hard to design directly; you remove what breaks it. |
| **U30** | **Cognitive Bias**: systematic thinking errors | Design honestly with biases (defaults, anchoring, social proof) **for** the user. Check every persuasive pattern against the dark-pattern list in `../cro.md`. | Using biases against users (confirmshaming, fake scarcity) is unethical and in the EU increasingly illegal (DSA Art. 25, consumer law). |
